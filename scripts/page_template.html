Surface water from headwaters to lagoon: rainfall, soil moisture and flooding

Rainfall, soil moisture and radar flooding, 2019–2024

Abstract

The Ogun River drains about {CATCH} km² of south-west Nigeria into the Lagos Lagoon. Rainfall and soil wetness are plausible controls on how much of its floodplain floods. We assembled a monthly satellite record for January 2019 to December 2024 of rainfall (CHIRPS), shallow soil moisture (ERA5-Land), radar floodplain inundation (Sentinel-1), an optical comparison series (Dynamic World) and lagoon turbidity (Sentinel-2), averaged over four sub-basins and a lagoon zone, and tested whether rainfall and soil moisture are associated with inundation, and inundation with turbidity, at lags of zero to two months for rainfall and soil moisture against inundation, and in the same month for inundation against turbidity. Associations were measured with Spearman's rho and autocorrelation-adjusted p-values, and the five headline relationships on month-to-month anomalies were corrected once for multiple testing. On raw monthly values, rainfall and inundation are negatively associated two months apart (ρ = {SEAS2_RHO}, q = {SEAS2_Q}), and soil moisture and inundation in the same month (ρ = {SM_RHO}, q = {SM_Q}); both are seasonal co-variations, and the negative sign and a February–March radar peak suggest that dry-season radar detections may drive them. This is not established. Once the seasonal cycle is removed, none of the headline relationships is significant (the largest, between inundation and lagoon turbidity, is ρ = {NDTI_RHO}, q = {NDTI_Q}). The radar index and the Dynamic World series agree in their seasonal pattern (ρ = {DW_SEAS_RHO}) but not in their anomalies (ρ = {DW_ANOM_RHO}). Dynamic World shows little water in the wet season, so it is not a reliable flood reference here, and the radar values are far smaller than its extent. Over six years, rainfall, soil moisture and radar inundation are seasonally related, with rainfall and radar inundation of opposite sign, but show no robust month-to-month coupling. The radar index is uncalibrated and should be treated with caution until it is validated.

1 Introduction

Floodplains store water and sediment between a river and its estuary. How much of a floodplain goes under water in a given month depends on rainfall, on how wet the soil already is, and on any reservoir upstream. Radar satellites can map inundation through cloud. The Sentinel-1 processing chain developed for automated flood mapping (Twele et al., 2016) is one such approach, and Dynamic World provides an optical land-cover record that includes open water and flooded vegetation (Brown et al., 2022).

In our review, limited evidence was found for a single satellite record that combines rainfall, soil moisture, floodplain inundation and lagoon turbidity at monthly resolution. This study asks whether such a record can show how rainfall and soil moisture relate to floodplain inundation, and whether inundation relates to turbidity in the lagoon, over 2019 to 2024.

2 Study area

The Ogun River drains about {CATCH} km² of south-west Nigeria. The catchment is divided into four hydrological sub-basins, Z1 to Z4, derived from HydroBASINS level 9 (Lehner & Grill, 2013). They run from the upland headwaters in the north, through the Oyan tributary and the mainstem, to an alluvial corridor on the lower floodplain. The fifth unit, Z5, is the part of a lagoon box at the river's outlet that lies outside the catchment. It is not a watershed, and it is used mainly for turbidity; rainfall and soil moisture are reported for it in the tables for completeness.

3 Methodology

  1. 1AcquireSentinel-1 radar, Sentinel-2 optical, CHIRPS rainfall, ERA5-Land soil moisture, Dynamic World land cover
  2. 2MaskKeep only ground with JRC occurrence below 80%, height above drainage below 15 m, slope below 5°
  3. 3ThresholdRadar water: VV below −16.5 dB and at least 3 dB darker than the January–March reference
  4. 4AggregateRadar is reduced at 120 m and Dynamic World at 60 m, rainfall and soil moisture at their native resolution, turbidity at 30 m; areas in km²; radar not interpreted for Z1 and Z2
  5. 5TestSpearman ρ on monthly values, adjusted for autocorrelation, with false-discovery-rate correction

Table 2 lists the data sources. Radar and Dynamic World are summed over Z3 and Z4 only, the two units in the export's floodplain domain. Radar values are not interpreted for Z1 and Z2, where dry-season radar artefacts make them unreliable; this follows the explorer.

3.1 Satellite data

All series come from Google Earth Engine (export v2.4.0), monthly from January 2019 to December 2024, giving {NMONTHS} months. Every published value was exported by script version 2.4.0, as recorded in each row of the monthly data file. The Earth Engine script in this repository is labelled 2.5.0 and has not been re-run on these exports, so the two versions are not yet reconciled. Rainfall is the Climate Hazards Group InfraRed Precipitation with Stations product (CHIRPS; Funk et al., 2015) at 0.05°, summed to monthly totals, and a month is kept only where at least five pentads are available. Shallow soil moisture is the volumetric water content of the 0–7 cm layer from ERA5-Land (Muñoz-Sabater et al., 2021) at 0.1°. The radar inundation index uses Sentinel-1 GRD backscatter in VV polarisation, ascending IW passes, with a focal filter of 50 m. The processing is related to the automated approach of Twele et al. (2016) but is not the same chain, and the index is not calibrated against gauge or optical water extent. Dynamic World V1 (Brown et al., 2022) gives an optical comparison series over the same floodplain domain, available in {NDW} months. Lagoon turbidity is the normalised difference turbidity index (NDTI) from Sentinel-2 surface reflectance over open water (modified NDWI above zero), which is available in {NNDTI} months. NDTI is an uncalibrated spectral indicator; it is not in NTU or mg/L. All {NNDTIMISS} missing months fall between June and September, the cloudy wet season.

The upstream reservoir is represented by the Sentinel-1 open-water area of Oyan Reservoir. This is a proxy for regulation, not a release record: no dam-release data were available.

3.2 Detection rules and reduction scales

Radar water is counted only where four conditions hold: VV backscatter is below −16.5 dB; it is at least 3 dB darker than the January–March reference for 2019–2024; the slope is below 5°; and the pixel is outside permanent water (JRC occurrence below 80%) and less than 15 m above the nearest drainage (MERIT Hydro). A fixed threshold alone may flag some dry-season ground as water, which is why the reference and the slope and drainage conditions are used. The monthly radar series is summed over the floodplain (Z3 and Z4) at 120 m. Dynamic World water and flooded-vegetation labels use the same permanent-water and drainage masks. Turbidity is the NDTI over open water (JRC occurrence of at least 50%), reduced at 30 m. These thresholds are choices made for this study, not values taken from the cited literature.

Two context layers are shown on the map, not used in any statistic. Terrain is the Copernicus DEM GLO-30 at 30 m, shown as a hillshade. Land cover is ESA WorldCover 2021 at 10 m, with an overall accuracy of 76.5% for Africa (Zanaga et al., 2022). Both are read through Microsoft Planetary Computer. Permanent water in the land-cover layer shows the Oyan Reservoir.

3.3 Spatial aggregation

Each variable is averaged over the pixels of its unit. Radar values are not used for Z1 and Z2, the upland units, following the rule applied in the explorer. Dynamic World and turbidity are available only where those units have valid observations, and the number of valid months is kept with every mean.

3.4 Seasonal cycle and anomalies

Two forms of each series are analysed. The raw monthly series show any seasonal co-variation. The anomaly series remove the seasonal cycle by subtracting, from each month, the mean for that calendar month across the six years. An anomaly therefore measures how much wetter, drier, more or less flooded than usual a given month was. With six years, each calendar month has only six values, so the climatology is itself uncertain.

3.5 Association and correction for multiple testing

Association is measured with Spearman's rank correlation (rho). Rho is used because the series are not normally distributed, and the diagnostics record that choice. Pearson's r and its confidence interval are reported for transparency, but they do not decide significance, because a single extreme month can dominate Pearson's r. Each p-value is adjusted for autocorrelation with an effective sample size. Pairs are tested at lags of zero, one and two months, so that a response within the same or the next two months can be seen.

The five headline relationships on anomalies form one family, and their p-values are corrected together with the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). The corrected value is reported as q. A relationship is called significant only if q is below 0.05.

3.6 Robustness

Robustness was tested for one pair only, rainfall against radar inundation at lag zero, by leaving out each month in turn and by removing the single month that most affects the result. The reservoir control was tested by removing the linear effect of reservoir area before testing rainfall against inundation.

3.7 Scope of inference

The analysis uses satellite proxies, not measurements. Rainfall is not groundwater recharge, and soil wetness in the top 7 cm is not groundwater availability. The study has no discharge observations, so it makes no claim about river flow. It has no groundwater observations, so it makes no claim about aquifers. Coastal position is not evidence of coastal vulnerability. The results are associations at monthly resolution, not causes.

4 Results

4.1 Seasonal pattern

Averaged by calendar month, rainfall peaks in {SEAS_PEAK_RAIN}, soil moisture in {SEAS_PEAK_SOIL} and the radar inundation index in {SEAS_PEAK_SAR}. The radar index also has a February–March peak, comparable to its October peak. The largest radar value is {SAR_MAX} km², in {PEAK_MONTH}. The optical Dynamic World series reaches {DW_MAX} km² in its largest month, so the two are on very different scales (Figure 3).

4.2 Associations on raw values and on anomalies

On raw monthly values, rainfall and radar inundation are negatively associated two months apart: rain two months earlier goes with less flooding now (ρ = {SEAS2_RHO}, q = {SEAS2_Q}). A one-month lag is borderline (ρ = {SEAS1_RHO}, q = {SEAS1_Q}). Soil moisture and flooding in the same month show a negative association too (ρ = {SM_RHO}, q = {SM_Q}). Two series that followed the same annual cycle would correlate positively; these correlations are negative. The most likely reading is that dry-season radar detections drive them, which would be an artefact rather than a hydrological link. Once the seasonal cycle is removed, none of them is significant (Figure 4). The seasonal relationships are therefore not evidence that rainfall drives flooding.

4.3 Month-to-month anomalies

With the seasonal cycle removed, rainfall and flooding in the same month show a weak, non-significant rank correlation (ρ = {RAIN_RHO}, q = {RAIN_Q}), and one month later (ρ = {RAIN1_RHO}, q = {RAIN1_Q}). Neither is significant after correction. The Pearson correlation for the same month is {RAIN_PEARSON}, with a 95% interval of {RAIN_PEARSON_LO} to {RAIN_PEARSON_HI}, but it depends on one month: leaving out {PEAK_MONTH} gives r = {ROB_R} (95% interval {ROB_LO} to {ROB_HI}). Soil moisture and flooding give ρ = {SM_ANOM_RHO} (q = {SM_ANOM_Q}). None of the {NFAM} headline relationships is significant after correction. Figure 5 shows the same comparison as scatter plots.

The Oyan Reservoir control leaves the same-month anomaly association almost unchanged (ρ = {CTL_RHO}, against {BASE_RHO} without the control). The reservoir proxy does not explain the absence of a month-to-month link. Because no release records were available, the regulation effect is only partly tested.

4.4 Flooding, lagoon turbidity and the optical reference

Lagoon turbidity and radar inundation anomalies show a weak, non-significant rank correlation (ρ = {NDTI_RHO}, q = {NDTI_Q}), the largest association among the headline relationships, but not significant after correction. The radar index ranks months like Dynamic World over the seasonal cycle (ρ = {DW_SEAS_RHO}), but not in anomalies (ρ = {DW_ANOM_RHO}, q = {DW_ANOM_Q}). Its values are far smaller than the optical extent, so its values should be read as uncalibrated detections, not as a measured flood area (Figure 3).

5 Wet–dry season contrasts and what a groundwater test would need

Figure 6 shows the four links the study measures or models, from rainfall to lagoon turbidity. Arrows give the order of the chain. They are not tested water transfers.

Table 3 compares wet and dry seasons for the units where the data can be read. Soil moisture is higher in the wet season in every unit. Dynamic World water is much higher in the dry season in every unit, including the floodplain units Z3 and Z4. In Z2 it averages {Z2_DW_DRY} km² in the dry season and {Z2_DW_WET} km² in the wet season; in Z1, {Z1_DW_DRY} km² and {Z1_DW_WET} km². Only water present in at least 80% of the JRC record is masked; seasonal water bodies, including reservoir margins, remain and may contribute. Possible explanations include few usable wet-season images and dry-season misclassification. The study cannot separate them, so Dynamic World does not show wet-season flooding here.

Where to look next for groundwater. Surface water that persists through the dry season would be the signal most worth testing against groundwater discharge. The present data cannot establish that persistence, because the dry-season Dynamic World signal is not validated. A test needs a validated persistent-water layer and dry-season water-level or spring observations, which this repository does not contain. No unit is ranked here.

6 Discussion

The raw correlations are negative and probably reflect dry-season radar detections rather than a shared cycle. Once the seasonal cycle is removed, no headline relationship survives correction. A link between rainfall and flooding is plausible from the seasonal pattern, but six years cannot show that it operates from month to month. Longer records are the first requirement, because they would give more years from which to estimate the seasonal cycle.

The radar index is the weakest part of the chain. Its seasonal pattern agrees with the optical reference (Dynamic World water), but its anomalies do not, and its magnitude is much smaller. Until it is calibrated against gauge or optical water extent, conclusions about flooding should be read as indicative.

7 Limitations

  • Six years give only six values per calendar month, so the anomalies depend on the six-year climatology and on individual years.
  • Monthly resolution is coarser than the travel time of water across the upland catchment. Lagged results may reflect storage in soil and floodplain rather than flood routing.
  • The radar inundation index is uncalibrated and far smaller than the optical reference. It is not interpreted for the upland units.
  • NDTI is an uncalibrated indicator. Ten of the 24 June-to-September months are missing, all cloud-related, so the core wet season is under-represented and the gaps are not random.
  • No dam-release records were available; the reservoir control uses only the open-water area of the reservoir.
  • CHIRPS (about 5 km) and ERA5-Land (about 9 km) are coarse relative to the floodplain of the sub-basins.
  • The autocorrelation adjustment is an approximation based on an effective sample size.

8 Conclusion

Over 2019–2024, rainfall and soil moisture follow the seasons in the Ogun catchment, but the radar index does not track them cleanly. Once the seasonal cycle is removed, no headline relationship is significant after correction. Dynamic World water is not a usable flood reference here. Testing month-to-month links needs a longer record and a calibrated flood extent.

Author’s note

I started this because I was excited by a simple question: could free satellite records show how rain, soil moisture and flooding move together, from the headwaters all the way to the lagoon? Getting the data into one consistent record took a lot of studying, from unfamiliar radar processing to the statistics of short, seasonal series, and I learned more from the parts that did not work than from the parts that did.

The seasons came through clearly. Over six years, though, the month-to-month links did not hold up under testing, and I would rather report that honestly than stretch it into something it is not. The radar index is the weakest part, because it is uncalibrated, and the page says so wherever it appears.

If you know this catchment and something here does not match what you see on the ground, I would really like to hear it, through the repository issues.
